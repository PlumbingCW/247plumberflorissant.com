High Water Bill and a Hidden Leak: A Florissant, MO Guide

When the bill climbs and nothing changed in the house, start at the meter.

By the Florissant 24/7 Plumbing team, 2026.

Call (314) 461-6239

Start with the meter test

Turn off every faucet and appliance that uses water. Look at the meter. If the leak indicator is spinning or the numbers advance in a few minutes, water is moving somewhere. Write down the reading, wait an hour with no use and read it again.

If the reading changed, you have a leak. If not, the cause may be a habit change, seasonal watering or a higher rate on the bill.

Where hidden leaks live

The toilet is the most common culprit. A few drops of food coloring in the tank will show color in the bowl if the flapper leaks.

  • Toilet flappers that let water trickle into the bowl.
  • Dripping faucets and showerheads.
  • Water heater relief valves that weep.
  • Slab or crawlspace supply lines.
  • The service line between the meter and the house.
  • Irrigation lines and outdoor spigots.

When to call for leak detection

If the meter moves and you cannot find the source, a plumber with acoustic and thermal tools can locate a leak behind a wall or under a floor. Our leak detection service does that, and if the leak is in the service line, water line repair covers the fix.

What to do about the extra charges

Ask your water utility whether it has a leak adjustment policy, and keep repair receipts and photos. Insurance may cover resulting damage but typically not the cost of the water itself, so check your own policy.

A meter test you can run in ten minutes

Take a photo of the meter before and after so you can show the numbers. This test separates a leak from a high-usage month, and it takes no tools.

  • Turn off every tap and appliance that uses water, including the ice maker and irrigation.
  • Find your water meter and look at the small leak indicator, often a triangle or a dial, for movement.
  • If it spins with everything off, note the reading and wait fifteen minutes without using water.
  • If the reading changed, water is leaving the house somewhere.

Where hidden leaks usually hide

The most common leak is a toilet that lets water slip from the tank into the bowl. A few drops of food coloring in the tank will show color in the bowl within minutes if the flapper is leaking. Next come dripping faucets, a water heater relief valve that weeps and irrigation lines buried in a yard.

Less often, the line from the meter to the house is the source. A wet patch in a dry stretch or a sound of water when no fixture is on can indicate it. For anything inside a wall, under a slab or in the yard, our leak detection service uses listening gear and thermal imaging so we find the spot before opening anything.

What to do about the bill itself

Fix the leak first, then call your water utility and ask about the billing period. Some utilities review a high bill after a documented repair, but policies vary, so ask yours what it needs. Keep the repair invoice and your meter photos.

If the bill is high and you find nothing, ask us for a leak check. A visit settles whether the cause is a leak or simply use.

Reading your bill

A bill shows usage over a billing period, and the number you want is the daily average. Compare the same months across years, since summer lawn watering and a houseguest will change the figure. A sudden jump with no change in how you live is the sign of a leak.

A steady leak shows up as higher use every period until it is fixed, while a one-time spike may point to a filling pool or an irrigation test. When you call us, have your last few bills handy, because the pattern helps us narrow the search.

Leak types and how big they get

Small leaks matter because they run all day. A leak that you can hear is often a toilet, and a leak that you cannot see is often under the yard. Both can be found and fixed quickly once someone looks.

Outdoor sources people forget

Hose bibs that drip, irrigation valves stuck open and a pool fill line left on all show up in the meter. Check each outdoor spigot, and look for wet patches or unusually green grass along buried lines. If you have an irrigation system, shut it off at the valve and see whether the meter stops.

A shutoff valve for outdoor lines in the basement helps in the fall, and it lets you drain the spigot lines before a freeze.

What to know in Florissant

Florissant's water is supplied by Missouri American Water, which has run the system since 2002 and in 2022 began replacing about 15,000 feet of cast iron mains installed between the 1940s and 1970s on Harrison, Washington and St. Catherine streets. A main replacement can involve service line transfers at the house, so ask the utility whether work near you could explain a meter or pressure change.

If the meter test shows a leak on your side, you own the line from the meter to the house. Confirm where the utility's responsibility ends before you dig.

Standards and sources
